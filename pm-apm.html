Analysis of Active Portfolio Management

Skill is scarce. This reading shows how much value a manager's edge can add per unit of risk, and how constraints leak it away.

Learning objectives this lesson covers (LOS)
  • LOS aDefine value added (active return) and relate it to active weights relative to a benchmark
  • LOS bCalculate the Sharpe ratio, information ratio, and their relationship to portfolio construction
  • LOS cState and interpret the fundamental law of active management, including breadth and information coefficient
  • LOS dExplain the transfer coefficient and how constraints reduce achievable active return
  • LOS eApply the fundamental law to strategies such as security selection and market timing

1. Value Added and Active Weights

Active management is the deliberate choice to hold portfolio weights that differ from a benchmark in the hope of outperforming it. The building block is the active weight on each security: the portfolio weight minus the benchmark weight. By construction, active weights sum to zero, so every overweight must be funded by an offsetting underweight.

The value added, or expected active return, is what those active bets are expected to earn. It equals the sum over all securities of each active weight multiplied by that security's expected active return (its expected return minus the benchmark's). A manager adds value by overweighting securities expected to beat the benchmark and underweighting those expected to lag.

Active return can be decomposed into two sources: return from asset allocation (active weights across asset classes) and return from security selection (active weights within a class). This lets an analyst attribute realized outperformance to the decisions that actually drove it.

Active weight_i = w_Pi - w_Bi
Value added = E(R_A) = SUM_i ( active weight_i x E(R_Ai) )
SUM TO ZEROActive weights always net to zero. Overweights and underweights are two sides of the same bet, financed against each other.

2. Sharpe Ratio vs Information Ratio

The Sharpe ratio judges a portfolio on a total-risk basis: excess return over the risk-free rate divided by the standard deviation of returns. It is unaffected by adding cash or leverage, which makes it useful for ranking standalone portfolios but blind to benchmark-relative performance.

The information ratio instead measures active return per unit of active risk (tracking error). It captures how efficiently a manager converts benchmark deviations into outperformance. Unlike the Sharpe ratio, an unconstrained manager's IR is unaffected by the aggressiveness of their active bets: scaling all active weights up or down changes active return and active risk proportionally, leaving the ratio fixed.

This scale-invariance is why the IR is the natural yardstick of skill. The manager first finds the position that maximizes IR, then dials the overall level of active risk up or down to hit a target, blending the active portfolio with the benchmark.

Sharpe ratio = ( R_P - R_f ) / STD(R_P)
IR = E(R_A) / STD(R_A) = active return / tracking error
WATCHAdding cash changes total risk but not the Sharpe ratio's ranking; it does shrink active risk and active return together, leaving IR unchanged for an unconstrained manager.

3. Constructing the Optimal Active Portfolio

The IR links directly to portfolio construction through the optimal level of active risk. For an unconstrained manager, the best amount of active risk to take is the one that maximizes the total Sharpe ratio of benchmark-plus-active-bets. That optimal active risk equals the manager's IR times the tracking error scaling that matches their risk tolerance.

A key result: an unconstrained manager who adds active bets to a benchmark can improve the squared Sharpe ratio of the combined portfolio by an amount equal to the square of the IR. So a higher IR is worth more not just in active space but in total risk-adjusted terms.

The practical takeaway is a two-step process. Maximize IR by forming the best possible forecast-driven active portfolio, then choose the level of aggressiveness (tracking error) separately based on the investor's appetite for active risk.

SR_P^2 = SR_B^2 + IR^2
Optimal active risk STD(R_A)* = ( IR / SR_B ) x STD(R_B)
TWO STEPSStep 1: maximize IR. Step 2: scale active risk to the client's tolerance. Skill and aggressiveness are separate decisions.

4. The Fundamental Law of Active Management

The fundamental law connects skill to results. It states that the information ratio a manager can achieve is roughly their information coefficient times the square root of breadth. IC measures the quality of each forecast; breadth counts how many truly independent forecasts are made per year.

The intuition is diversification of insight. A modest edge applied to many independent decisions compounds into a strong IR, because the noise in individual forecasts partly cancels while the skill accumulates. The square root reflects that doubling breadth does not double IR; it multiplies it by about 1.41.

Two levers raise expected active return: sharpen forecasts (raise IC) or find more independent opportunities to apply them (raise BR). Crucially, breadth requires independent decisions. Betting the same macro view across 100 correlated stocks is closer to one decision than one hundred.

IR = IC x sqrt(BR)
E(R_A)* = IC x sqrt(BR) x STD(R_A)
INDEPENDENCEBreadth counts INDEPENDENT bets. Highly correlated positions inflate the count but not the real breadth, overstating expected IR.

5. Transfer Coefficient and Constraints

The basic law assumes an unconstrained manager who can translate every forecast into an ideal position. Real portfolios face limits: no short selling, position caps, sector bounds, turnover budgets. These constraints stop the manager from fully expressing their views.

The transfer coefficient captures this slippage. It is the correlation between the active weights actually held and the active weights the forecasts would ideally imply. A TC of 1.0 means perfect implementation; a TC of 0.0 means the positions bear no relation to the insights. Realistic long-only portfolios often sit around 0.3 to 0.6.

The full law scales the achievable IR by the transfer coefficient. Because expected active return is proportional to IR, a TC of 0.5 throws away half the value the manager's skill could have produced. Constraints are not free; they are a direct tax on active return.

IR = TC x IC x sqrt(BR)
E(R_A) = TC x IC x sqrt(BR) x STD(R_A)
THE LEAKTC below 1 means good forecasts are only partly implemented. Loosening a binding constraint raises TC and recovers active return.

6. Applying the Law: Selection and Timing

For security selection, breadth is large. A manager who independently ranks, say, 200 stocks and rebalances quarterly generates high BR, so even a small IC can produce a respectable IR. This is why broad, disciplined stock-picking strategies can succeed with only a slight per-name edge.

For market timing, breadth is tiny. Making an independent bull/bear call on an index a few times a year gives very low BR. To reach the same IR as a diversified stock selector, a market timer needs a far higher IC, which is why consistent timing skill is so rare and hard to prove.

When decisions repeat over time, breadth can be estimated as the number of bets per year times the years, adjusted for any correlation between successive calls. Positively correlated calls reduce effective breadth below the raw count, tempering the IR the strategy can realistically deliver.

BR (timing) small -> needs high IC
BR (selection) large -> small IC suffices
TIMINGMarket timers have low breadth, so they need exceptional forecasting skill (high IC) to match a diversified stock selector's IR.
