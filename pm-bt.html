Backtesting & Simulation

Backtesting asks: would this strategy have worked? Simulation asks: could it work under conditions history never showed us?

Key Terms

TermMeaning
BacktestingApplying an investment rule to historical data to estimate how a strategy would have performed out-of-sample in the past.
Look-ahead biasUsing data in a backtest that was not actually available or knowable at the decision date, e.g. as-reported earnings before release.
Survivorship biasTesting only on securities that still exist today, omitting delisted/failed names, which inflates returns and understates risk.
Data snoopingOver-mining the same data to find rules that fit past noise; also called overfitting or p-hacking, producing spurious signals.
Cross-validationPartitioning data into training and validation subsets to tune and test a model on data not used to build it.
Rolling-window backtestRepeatedly re-estimating and re-forming the portfolio over a sliding fixed-length window that moves forward through time.
Monte Carlo simulationGenerating many random return paths from an assumed distribution to estimate the full range of possible strategy outcomes.

1. Objectives, Steps & Process

Backtesting estimates how an investment strategy would have performed had it been implemented in the past. Its core objective is to build confidence that a rule captures a genuine, repeatable edge rather than luck, and to understand the strategy's risk before committing capital.

The standard process runs in stages: strategy design (define the investment universe, signal, rebalancing frequency, and constraints); historical investment simulation (form the portfolio period by period using only information available then); and performance analysis (compute returns, risk, and factor exposures).

A key design choice is rebalancing. A rolling-window approach re-estimates the model and reconstitutes the portfolio each period, mimicking how a manager would actually update decisions as new data arrives.

2. Interpreting Metrics & Visuals

Judge a factor strategy on risk-adjusted terms, not raw return. The Sharpe ratio measures excess return per unit of total volatility; the information ratio measures active return per unit of tracking error versus a benchmark. Also inspect maximum drawdown (worst peak-to-trough loss) and downside deviation.

Return distributions matter: negative skew and high kurtosis warn that averages hide fat-tailed loss risk. A high mean with deep drawdowns and left-skew is more fragile than a modestly lower, symmetric one.

Common visuals: cumulative return curves (smoothness signals consistency), rolling Sharpe/IR plots (is the edge stable or decaying?), drawdown charts, and factor-return correlation heat maps to check whether apparent alpha is really disguised known-factor beta.

Sharpe = (Rp - Rf) / sigma_p
IR = (Rp - Rb) / TrackingError
RISK FIRSTTwo strategies with equal mean return can differ hugely in drawdown and skew; always rank on risk-adjusted and tail metrics.

3. Pitfalls: The Big Three Biases

Survivorship bias: building the test universe from securities that exist today drops firms that went bankrupt, delisted, or merged away. Because losers are removed, backtested returns look too high and risk too low. Fix: use a point-in-time database that includes dead securities.

Look-ahead bias: using information in a decision that was not yet available at that date. Examples: financial statements before their filing date, restated data, or benchmark constituents defined with hindsight. Fix: lag data to its true availability and use as-first-reported values.

Data snooping / overfitting: testing thousands of variations until one fits past noise. The winning rule then fails live. Fix: reserve untouched out-of-sample data, limit the number of trials, and demand economic rationale for the signal.

LOOK-AHEAD vs SURVIVORSHIPSurvivorship = WRONG sample (dead names dropped). Look-ahead = RIGHT sample but using data too early (not yet knowable at the decision date).

4. Cross-Validation & Rolling Windows

Because a single train/test split can be lucky, cross-validation repeatedly rotates which data trains the model and which validates it, then averages performance. This gives a more stable estimate of out-of-sample skill and helps detect overfitting.

With time-series data, ordinary random k-fold splitting can leak the future into the past. Use time-aware schemes instead: always train on earlier data and validate on strictly later data.

Two rolling schemes: a walk-forward (expanding) window keeps adding new data to a growing training set, while a fixed rolling window keeps the training length constant, dropping the oldest observations. Fixed windows adapt faster to regime change; expanding windows use more data and are more stable.

Walk-forward: train[t0..t], test[t+1]; then slide t forward

5. Historical vs Monte Carlo Simulation

Historical simulation resamples or replays actual past returns, so it preserves the real, observed dependence, skew, and fat tails of markets. Its weakness: it is limited to scenarios that actually happened and can be dominated by one unusual episode.

Monte Carlo simulation draws returns from an assumed statistical distribution (often with specified means, volatilities, and correlations), generating thousands of hypothetical paths. It can explore outcomes history never produced and stress rare events, but its results are only as valid as the assumed distribution.

Use historical simulation when you trust that the past sample represents future dynamics; use Monte Carlo when you need a richer range of outcomes or want to inject specific tail or correlation assumptions.

CORE DIFFERENCEHistorical = replay real data (no distribution assumed). Monte Carlo = generate synthetic paths from an assumed distribution.

6. Sensitivity Analysis of Distribution Assumptions

A Monte Carlo result is conditional on its inputs, so robust analysis varies those inputs. In sensitivity analysis you re-run the simulation while altering the assumed return distribution and observe how conclusions shift.

Typical changes: raise volatility, shift the mean lower, increase correlations toward 1 (diversification vanishes in crises), or swap a normal distribution for a fat-tailed one such as Student's t to capture extreme moves.

If the strategy's Sharpe, drawdown, or shortfall probability stay acceptable across these altered assumptions, conclusions are robust. If modest changes flip the verdict, the strategy is fragile and the base-case backtest overstated its reliability.
