Exchange-Traded Funds: Mechanics & Applications

ETFs trade like stocks but a creation/redemption arbitrage loop keeps their price tethered to NAV cheaply and tax-efficiently.

1. Primary Market: Creation & Redemption

ETF shares are created and destroyed in the primary market through authorized participants (APs), not by ordinary investors. To create shares, an AP delivers a specified creation basket of securities (and/or cash) to the sponsor and receives a creation unit of new ETF shares in return. Redemption reverses this: the AP hands back a creation unit and receives the redemption basket of underlying securities.

Because most transfers are in-kind (securities for shares, not cash), the fund rarely sells holdings to meet flows. This open-ended, elastic supply of shares is what lets the ETF price stay anchored to value.

APs are compensated by arbitrage profit and fees, not by the sponsor directly. They bear the market risk of holding baskets briefly.

KEYOnly APs deal in the primary market. Retail investors always trade existing shares on the exchange (secondary market).

2. Primary-Market Arbitrage: Anchoring Price to NAV

The creation/redemption channel is a self-correcting arbitrage loop. If the ETF trades at a premium (price > NAV), an AP buys the cheaper underlying basket, creates new shares, and sells them at the higher market price - pocketing the gap. This new supply pushes the price down toward NAV.

If the ETF trades at a discount (price < NAV), the AP buys cheap ETF shares in the market, redeems them for the more valuable basket, and sells the securities. This demand pushes the price up toward NAV.

Arbitrage is profitable only when the gap exceeds the AP's transaction costs, so a small persistent band around NAV is normal, especially for less liquid or foreign holdings.

Premium/Discount % = (Price - NAV) / NAV

3. Secondary Market & Sources of Tracking Error

Most ETF volume is secondary-market trading between investors; no fund cash flows occur, so one investor's sale needs another's purchase. Secondary liquidity plus the threat of primary arbitrage keeps quoted prices near iNAV.

Tracking error (return dispersion vs the index) and tracking difference (the cumulative return gap) arise from: the expense ratio dragging returns; imperfect sampling/optimization instead of full replication; cash drag; rebalancing and transaction costs; dividend reinvestment timing; index changes; withholding taxes; and any securities-lending income (which can offset the gap).

Premium/discount also widens when the underlying market is closed (e.g., an international ETF trading while foreign exchanges are shut) or is illiquid, because iNAV becomes stale.

Tracking difference = ETF return - Index return
NUANCETracking difference is the size of the gap; tracking error is its volatility. Exam questions often test the distinction.

4. Costs & Tax Efficiency

An ETF's total cost of ownership has two buckets: recurring ongoing costs (management/expense ratio, plus any tracking drag) that scale with the holding period, and one-time trading costs (bid-ask spread, commissions, and the premium/discount paid on entry/exit). For short holding periods trading costs dominate, so spread and liquidity matter most; for long horizons the expense ratio dominates.

ETFs are generally more tax-efficient than mutual funds. In-kind redemptions let the fund hand out low-basis securities to the AP, purging embedded gains without a taxable sale, so fewer capital-gains distributions flow to shareholders. Secondary-market trading also insulates continuing holders from other investors' redemptions.

Total holding cost ~= Expense ratio x (days/365) + round-trip trading costs
TRAPA low expense ratio does not guarantee low total cost - a wide bid-ask spread can dominate for a short-term trader.

5. ETF Risks

Counterparty risk is highest in synthetic ETFs that gain exposure via total-return swaps: if the swap counterparty defaults, tracking can break down. Physical ETFs engaging in securities lending carry a smaller version of this risk against the borrower/collateral.

Fund-closure risk: small or unprofitable ETFs can be liquidated, forcing an untimely taxable exit and reinvestment. Watch tiny AUM, thin volume, or a crowded niche.

Expectation-related risk: investors misunderstand what they hold - e.g., leveraged/inverse ETFs reset daily and compound, so multi-day returns diverge from the stated multiple; commodity ETFs using futures suffer roll costs in contango. Also note market-maker/AP concentration risk: if the few APs step back in stress, arbitrage weakens and discounts widen.

SYNTHETICSwap-based (synthetic) ETFs add counterparty risk but can track hard-to-access markets more tightly.

6. Portfolio Applications

ETFs are versatile tools for asset allocation: cheap, liquid building blocks to assemble strategic exposures across equities, bonds, sectors, factors, or countries.

For rebalancing and cash equitization, ETFs let a manager quickly restore target weights or put idle cash to work at low cost without picking individual names. They enable tactical tilts - overweighting a sector, style, or theme - and can be shorted or used for hedging.

Other uses: transition management as interim exposure while hiring managers, liquidity management in fixed income where the ETF often trades more liquidly than its bonds, and tax-loss harvesting by swapping into a similar-but-not-identical ETF.
