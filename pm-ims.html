Investment Manager Selection

Picking a manager is hypothesis testing: is there real skill? Get the error type wrong and you fire winners or hire pretenders.

1. Components and Objectives of Selection

The goal of manager selection is not simply to find the best past performer but to identify a manager whose expected future results, adjusted for risk and cost, best fit a specific mandate. Strong past returns are noisy and easily produced by luck, so selection combines statistical evidence with judgment.

The process has three linked stages. First, the universe is defined by the mandate (asset class, style, constraints). Second, a quantitative stage screens performance, risk, and style consistency. Third, a qualitative stage conducts due diligence on philosophy, process, people, and operations. Quantitative work says what happened; qualitative work explains why and whether it is repeatable.

FIT OVER FAMESelection optimizes mandate fit and repeatable skill, not headline returns. A top performer that drifts from the mandate is a poor selection.

2. Type I vs Type II Errors

Frame hiring as a hypothesis test where the null is the manager has no skill. A Type I error is wrongly rejecting a true null: you conclude skill exists and hire (or retain) a manager who in fact has none. A Type II error is failing to reject a false null: you conclude no skill and reject (or fire) a manager who truly does have skill.

Both errors carry real costs. Type I brings underperformance, fees, and eventual disruption from replacing the manager. Type II brings the opportunity cost of forgone alpha plus the transaction and search costs of switching. Tightening standards to cut one error type typically raises the other, so committees must weigh the relative costs rather than eliminate errors.

H0: no skill
Type I = reject true H0 = HIRE a no-skill manager
Type II = accept false H0 = FIRE a skilled manager
DON'T FLIPType I = hiring the pretender (false positive on skill). Type II = firing the star (false negative). Anchor to the null 'no skill' to avoid reversing them.

3. Return-Based Style Analysis

RBSA regresses a fund's historical returns against a set of style indices, constraining the coefficients to be non-negative and to sum to one, so they read as an effective style mix. It needs only a return series, applies uniformly across managers, and is inexpensive and quick to run.

Its weaknesses follow from using only returns. It is backward-looking, blends exposures over the estimation window, and can lag real shifts or misread a manager whose true holdings are unusual. Low explanatory power (R2) or unstable coefficients can flag style drift or benchmark mismatch.

r_fund = a + b_1*I_1 + ... + b_n*I_n + e
subject to: b_i >= 0 and sum(b_i) = 1

4. Holdings-Based Style Analysis

HBSA classifies the actual securities in the portfolio (by size, value/growth metrics, sector, credit, duration) and aggregates them to a current style profile. Because it uses positions, it is forward-consistent and precise, detects drift faster, and pinpoints the sources of exposure.

The trade-offs are practical: it demands frequent, detailed holdings data that may be disclosed with a lag, is computationally heavier, and depends on a consistent classification scheme, making cross-manager comparison harder. In practice RBSA and HBSA are complementary checks.

PAIR THEMRBSA is cheap and uniform but stale; HBSA is precise and current but data-hungry. Agreement between them raises confidence; divergence flags a problem.

5. Qualitative Due Diligence

Due diligence tests whether observed results reflect a sound, repeatable approach. Review the investment philosophy (the economic rationale for the edge), the process (idea generation, portfolio construction, risk controls, sell discipline, and whether it is actually followed), and the people (experience, depth, incentives, ownership, and key-person risk).

Operations cover the non-investment risks that can be catastrophic: custody and asset safekeeping, independent valuation, compliance and controls, trading and counterparties, technology, and business viability. A brilliant strategy on a weak operational platform is not investable, so operational red flags can veto an otherwise strong candidate.

OPS CAN VETOThe four P's plus operations. Weak controls, self-valuation, or key-person risk can disqualify a manager regardless of returns.

6. Fees and Alignment of Interests

Fee structure shapes incentives. An ad valorem (assets-under-management) fee is predictable for clients but rewards asset gathering over performance. A performance-based fee pays a base plus a share of returns above a hurdle, better aligning pay with results but potentially encouraging risk-taking.

Alignment tools temper that risk. A high-water mark stops the manager from earning incentive fees on gains that merely recover prior losses. A hurdle rate requires beating a threshold before sharing. Symmetric (fulcrum) fees that penalize underperformance, along with fee caps and the manager investing personal capital, further align interests. Lower, simpler fees also raise the after-cost return the client keeps.

Perf fee = base + k * max(0, R - hurdle)
High-water mark: incentive paid only on new peak NAV
NET IS KINGCompare managers on expected net-of-fee, risk-adjusted return. High-water marks and hurdles protect clients from paying for recovered losses.
