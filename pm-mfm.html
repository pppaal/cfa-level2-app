Using Multifactor Models

One factor rarely explains a portfolio. Learn to price, attribute, and manage risk across many systematic drivers.

Key Terms

TermMeaning
Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT)Model expressing expected return as a linear function of a risk-free rate plus multiple systematic factor risk premia.
Factor risk premium (lambda)Expected extra return per unit of sensitivity to a systematic factor; the reward for bearing that factor's risk.
Factor sensitivity (beta)The responsiveness of an asset's return to a one-unit change in a given factor, holding others constant.
Arbitrage opportunityA trade needing no net investment and no risk that still earns a positive expected return; APT assumes these are eliminated.
Macroeconomic factor modelModel whose factors are surprises in economic variables such as GDP growth, inflation, or credit spreads.
Fundamental factor modelModel whose factors are firm attributes (size, value, momentum); sensitivities are the standardized attributes themselves.
Statistical factor modelModel that extracts unobservable factors from return data via techniques like factor or principal-components analysis.
Active risk (tracking error)Standard deviation of the difference between portfolio and benchmark returns; decomposed into factor and asset-specific parts.
Information ratioActive return divided by active risk; measures value added per unit of tracking error in active management.

1. Arbitrage Pricing Theory and Its Assumptions

APT extends single-factor pricing by holding that an asset's expected return is a linear combination of its exposures to several systematic (priced) factors. It does not identify the factors, only the pricing structure they impose.

APT rests on three assumptions: (1) asset returns are generated by a multifactor process; (2) there are enough assets that firm-specific (idiosyncratic) risk can be diversified away; and (3) no arbitrage opportunities exist across well-diversified portfolios.

Unlike the CAPM, APT does not require a mean-variance efficient market portfolio, normally distributed returns, or investor utility assumptions. Each factor carries a risk premium (lambda) equal to the return on a portfolio with unit sensitivity to that factor and zero sensitivity to all others.

E(R_p) = Rf + b_p1*L1 + b_p2*L2 + ... + b_pk*Lk
CAPM IS A SPECIAL CASECAPM is APT with a single factor (the market). APT generalizes it to k priced systematic factors.

2. Computing Expected Return from a Multifactor Model

To find an expected return, multiply each factor sensitivity by its factor risk premium, sum the products, and add the risk-free rate. Premia can be negative when investors will accept lower returns for exposure (e.g., a hedge against inflation).

Worked example. Rf = 3%. Factor 1 (GDP surprise): b1 = 1.5, L1 = 4%. Factor 2 (inflation surprise): b2 = 0.8, L2 = -2%.
E(R) = 3 + (1.5)(4) + (0.8)(-2) = 3 + 6 - 1.6 = 7.4%.

APT also prices well-diversified portfolios: if two portfolios have identical factor sensitivities they must offer identical expected returns, or an arbitrage exists. This constraint is what pins down each lambda.

E(R) = 3% + 1.5(4%) + 0.8(-2%) = 7.4%
SIGN OF THE PREMIUMA negative factor premium lowers expected return when sensitivity is positive. Do not drop the minus sign when summing.

3. Three Families of Multifactor Models

Macroeconomic factor models use surprises (actual minus expected) in variables like GDP growth, inflation, interest rates, or credit spreads as factors. Sensitivities are estimated by regressing asset returns on these surprises; the intercept approximates the expected return.

Fundamental factor models use firm or security attributes such as size, book-to-market (value), leverage, or momentum. Here the roles reverse: the attributes are the (standardized) sensitivities, and the factor returns are estimated cross-sectionally by regression.

Statistical factor models apply factor analysis or principal components to historical returns to build factors that best explain return covariances. They fit data well but the factors lack an inherent economic interpretation and may not be stable out of sample.

The Carhart four-factor model is a widely used fundamental model: market excess return (RMRF), size (SMB), value (HML), and momentum (WML).

E(R) = Rf + b_mkt*RMRF + b_smb*SMB + b_hml*HML + b_wml*WML
WHO IS THE SENSITIVITY?Macro models: sensitivities are estimated (regression slopes). Fundamental models: sensitivities are the observed standardized attributes.

4. Return Attribution and Factor Exposures

A multifactor model decomposes an active return into a sum of factor return contributions (each active factor exposure times that factor's realized return) plus a security-selection (asset-specific) return that the factors do not explain.

Active factor exposure equals the portfolio's sensitivity minus the benchmark's sensitivity to each factor. Positive active exposure to a factor that performed well adds to active return; the leftover is attributed to selection skill.

This isolates whether a manager's outperformance came from deliberate factor tilts (e.g., overweighting value) or from picking individual securities within factor-neutral positioning.

Active return = SUM[(b_p - b_b)*F] + Security selection

5. Risk Analysis with Factor Models

A factor model splits total return variance into systematic (factor) risk and idiosyncratic (asset-specific) risk. For a diversified portfolio, factor risk dominates because unsystematic risk nets out across holdings.

Active risk (tracking error) is likewise decomposed into active factor risk (from active factor exposures) and active specific risk (from active security weights). Managers use this to check that risk is being taken where they intend.

Comparing active return to active risk gives the information ratio, the standard measure of risk-adjusted active performance.

Active risk^2 = Active factor risk^2 + Active specific risk^2
VARIANCES ADDBecause factor and specific components are independent, their variances (squared risks) add, not the standard deviations.

6. Uses in Active Management and Portfolio Construction

Active management. Factor models let managers express views as controlled factor tilts and measure the resulting active return and information ratio, separating factor bets from security selection.

Portfolio construction. Managers build factor-mimicking portfolios to gain a targeted unit exposure to one factor while neutralizing others, enabling tilts, hedges, or benchmark tracking. A passive index fund is built to match the benchmark's factor exposures with near-zero active risk.

Risk analysis. Beyond measurement, factor models support scenario and stress testing: apply hypothetical factor shocks to the exposures to estimate portfolio impact, and rebalance to keep exposures within limits.

MULTIFACTOR EDGEMultifactor models control multiple risk dimensions at once, something a single-factor (market) model cannot do.
