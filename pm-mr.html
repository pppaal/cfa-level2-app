Measuring & Managing Market Risk

One number to summarize downside risk - and everything it hides. Master VaR, its three estimation methods, its cousins, and the sensitivities beneath.

Key Terms

TermMeaning
Value at Risk (VaR)Minimum loss expected over a set horizon at a given probability (e.g., 5% monthly VaR = worst loss in best 95% of months).
Parametric VaRAnalytical VaR assuming returns are normally distributed, using portfolio mean and standard deviation.
Historical SimulationVaR from re-pricing the current portfolio over actual past returns; no distributional assumption.
Monte Carlo SimulationVaR from thousands of randomly generated return paths using assumed statistical processes for risk factors.
Conditional VaR (CVaR)Expected loss GIVEN that the loss exceeds the VaR threshold; also called expected shortfall or expected tail loss.
Incremental VaRChange in portfolio VaR from adding or removing a position (or changing its size).
Marginal VaRChange in VaR for a small (one-unit) change in a position; sensitivity of VaR to that holding.

Sensitivity MeasureEstimate of how an asset's value changes given a move in an underlying risk factor (beta, duration, delta).

1. What VaR Is - and Its Three Parameters

VaR answers: over the next period, what is the minimum loss we expect with a given probability? Every VaR statement has three ingredients: a loss size (currency or %), a time horizon (day, month), and a probability (5%, 1%).

Read it precisely: a 5% monthly VaR of $4 million means there is a 5% chance the portfolio loses at least $4M in a month - equivalently, in 95% of months the loss is smaller than $4M. VaR is a minimum loss at that tail, not the maximum or the average loss.

Confidence level = 1 - probability. A 5% VaR and a 95%-confidence VaR are the same thing. Lower probability (1% vs 5%) sits further in the tail, so the VaR number is larger.

5% VaR = $4M <=> P(Loss >= $4M) = 5%
NOT THE WORST CASEVaR is the FLOOR of tail losses, not the ceiling. Losses beyond VaR can be far larger - that is exactly what VaR fails to tell you.

2. Parametric (Variance-Covariance) VaR

The parametric method assumes returns are normally distributed and computes VaR analytically from the portfolio's expected return and standard deviation. Pick a z-score for the tail: 1.65 for 5%, 2.33 for 1%.

VaR as a positive loss = (z*sigma - mu) x Portfolio Value. For short horizons mu is often assumed zero. Scale a daily sigma to T days by sqrt(T).

Strengths: simple, fast, ideal for linear, normally-behaved assets. Weakness: normality understates tail risk (real returns have fat tails), and it handles options poorly because delta is only a linear approximation of a curved payoff.

VaR = [ z*sigma_P - E(R_P) ] * V
z(5%)=1.65 z(1%)=2.33
Example: (1.65*0.20 - 0.10)*100M = 23M
MEAN MATTERSSubtracting a positive expected return LOWERS VaR. If given a nonzero mean, use it - ignoring it overstates the loss.

3. Historical vs Monte Carlo Simulation

Historical simulation applies the current portfolio to a window of actual past risk-factor changes, builds the loss distribution, and reads VaR off the chosen percentile. No normality assumption and it captures real fat tails - but it assumes the past repeats and is limited by the length/quality of the data window.

Monte Carlo simulation generates thousands of random scenarios from assumed statistical processes for the risk factors, revalues the portfolio in each, and takes the percentile. It is the most flexible - it handles nonlinear options and complex distributions - but is computationally intensive and only as good as its input assumptions (model risk).

Neither simulation method needs the normal distribution; only the parametric method does. Historical uses real data; Monte Carlo uses generated data.

MATCH THE METHODOptions-heavy or nonlinear book -> Monte Carlo or full-revaluation historical, NOT parametric. Parametric's linear delta breaks on convex payoffs.

4. Limitations and Extensions of VaR

Limitations: VaR is subjective (which method/parameters?), sensitive to those choices, ignores the size of losses beyond the threshold, and - measured under normal conditions - can lull users into a false sense of safety. It can also underestimate correlated tail events, since correlations tend toward 1 in crises.

Conditional VaR (CVaR) fixes the 'how bad beyond the tail' blind spot: it is the average loss conditional on breaching VaR. CVaR is greater than or equal to VaR always.

Marginal and incremental VaR attribute risk to positions. Incremental VaR = portfolio VaR with the position minus VaR without it - the risk that position adds. Marginal VaR is the per-unit sensitivity of VaR to a holding, useful for optimization. Relative VaR (ex-ante tracking error scaled) measures loss versus a benchmark.

CVaR = E[ Loss | Loss > VaR ] ( CVaR >= VaR )
Incremental VaR = VaR(with) - VaR(without)
COHERENTCVaR is subadditive (diversification never raises it), a property VaR can violate. That is why regulators lean toward expected shortfall.

5. Sensitivity Measures by Asset Class

Equity - beta: sensitivity of a stock/portfolio return to the market return. It scales systematic exposure but says nothing about the size of a market move.

Fixed income - duration and convexity: duration gives the first-order (linear) price response to a yield change; convexity is the second-order curvature correction. Together: %change_P ~= -D*dy + 0.5*C*(dy)^2.

Options - the Greeks: delta = sensitivity to underlying price (first order), gamma = sensitivity of delta to price (curvature), vega = sensitivity to volatility, theta = time decay, rho = interest-rate sensitivity. Delta plus gamma parallel duration plus convexity - a linear term plus a curvature term.

Sensitivity measures give a local risk picture (response to a move) whereas VaR gives a probabilistic loss magnitude. They are complements, not substitutes.

%dP ~= -D*dy + 0.5*C*(dy)^2
dOption ~= Delta*dS + 0.5*Gamma*(dS)^2
DIRECTIONConvexity/gamma is the SECOND-order term. Duration alone overstates losses on big rate rises and understates gains - convexity corrects both.

6. Scenario Analysis and Stress Testing

VaR describes normal markets; scenario analysis and stress testing probe the tail. Historical scenarios replay real crises (1987 crash, 2008) through today's portfolio. Hypothetical scenarios construct plausible-but-unseen shocks.

Stress testing can be a single-factor 'stressed VaR' (parameters set to crisis levels) or a full multi-factor scenario. Reverse stress testing starts from a portfolio-killing loss and works backward to find which scenarios produce it.

These techniques catch what VaR misses: extreme moves, correlation breakdowns, and liquidity droughts. Regulators require them precisely because a clean VaR number can hide fragility.

COMPLEMENTStress tests are not probabilistic - they say 'if X happens, we lose Y.' They answer magnitude under extremes, where VaR is silent.

7. How Different Participants Use Risk Measures

Banks: care about liquidity and leverage; use VaR, stressed VaR, and enterprise-wide sensitivity limits for regulatory capital (Basel) and to monitor asset-liability mismatches across trading desks.

Pension funds: focus on funded status and the surplus (assets minus liabilities). They use surplus-at-risk and liability-relative (tracking-error / relative VaR) measures over long horizons, with policy allocation constraints.

Insurers: distinguish P&C from life. They emphasize economic capital, asset-liability matching, and scenario testing tied to policyholder obligations; VaR supports solvency (e.g., Solvency II) frameworks.

Asset managers: emphasize relative risk versus a benchmark (ex-ante tracking error, active-share and position limits) and absolute VaR for total-return/hedge strategies, plus redemption/liquidity risk.

RELATIVE vs ABSOLUTEPensions and index managers watch risk RELATIVE to liabilities/benchmark; banks and hedge funds watch ABSOLUTE loss. Match the measure to the mandate.
